The Long View

Nobody wants to ask this one first.

So here is the answer before you have to.

{{FirstName}}, most people spend months researching a renovation without ever getting a straight number, because asking feels like committing. Here is the public data for our part of Maryland, and then the part that actually decides where you land inside it.

Construction cost only. Design fees, permits, furnishings and landscaping sit outside these figures.

A sunroom costs more per square foot than a bedroom for the same reason a kitchen costs more than a den: glass, structure and mechanical systems, not floor area. Square footage is the worst predictor of price in the whole exercise.

What actually moves your number

— How much of the existing house you touch. Tying into an old roof line or moving a bearing wall costs more than building outward into open air.
— Site access. A crew that cannot get equipment to the back of the house pays for that in labour, every day.
— What is behind the walls. Knob-and-tube, undersized panels, failing cast iron — found in week two, paid for in week ten.
— Finish level. The same room, done two ways, can differ by a factor of three.
— How settled your decisions are. Changes on paper are free. Changes on site are not.

The fee question

An architect is the cheapest part of the budget and the one with the most leverage on it.

Design fees on residential work are commonly a percentage of construction cost, and they buy you three things that pay for themselves: a scope contractors can bid identically, so you can actually compare their numbers; drawings that survive permit review; and someone whose only interest during construction is yours.

If you want a realistic range for your specific house rather than a national average, that is exactly what the first conversation produces.
