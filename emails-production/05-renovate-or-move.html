The Long View

Renovate, or move?

Almost nobody is really asking about money.

{{FirstName}}, in forty years I have watched a lot of couples have this argument at the kitchen table, and it almost always gets framed as a budget question. It isn’t.

The honest question is narrower, and it has two parts: can the bones of this house give you what you want, and will the lot let you build it? If the answer to both is yes, renovating usually wins. If either is a hard no, you should move — and I will tell you so.

Renovating tends to win when

— You love the street, the schools or the commute more than you dislike the floor plan.
— The problem is layout, not size — walls in the wrong place, light in the wrong rooms.
— The lot has room in the right direction, and zoning allows it.
— The structure is sound and the systems are not all at end of life at once.

Moving tends to win when

— You need substantially more house than the lot will legally hold.
— The foundation, or the whole envelope, is the actual problem.
— You would be the most expensive house on the block by a wide margin.
— You need to be somewhere else — a different school district, a shorter drive, one level.

The number nobody puts in the spreadsheet

Moving costs real money and buys you no new space.

Between agent commission, transfer and recordation taxes, closing costs and the move itself, selling and buying commonly runs somewhere around eight to ten per cent of a home’s value. On a $600,000 house that is a serious sum spent on transacting rather than on rooms. It is not an argument for renovating — sometimes moving is plainly right — but it belongs on the same page as the construction estimate.

If you want a second opinion on which side your house falls, just reply and tell me which way you are leaning and why. I will tell you honestly if I think you are wrong.
