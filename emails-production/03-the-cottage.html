Selected Work

The smallest building on the property took the most drawings.

{{FirstName}}, this is a two-bedroom guest cottage. It is roughly the size of the addition most people write to us about.

It also took longer to resolve than anything else on the site, and that is not a complaint — it is how small buildings work. In a large house a mistake has room to hide. In a small one, every window is doing three jobs at once, and if the pergola sits four inches too low the whole thing looks wrong forever.

01  —  The approach
Two dormers, a bay, and a pergola that carries the eye along the front instead of letting it stop at the door. The cottage is smaller than it looks, on purpose.

02 — The garden side

  

03 — The water side
Every side of a small building is a front. There is nowhere to put the ugly elevation, so there isn’t one.
 

04 — Inside

Look at the floor.

There is a dark inlaid border curving through the oak to follow the line of the arch above it. Nothing structural required that. It exists because somebody drew it, and because the floor layer was handed a drawing that said exactly where the line went.

Why this one and not the big house

Small projects are not simpler. They are less forgiving.

People sometimes assume an architect is for the big job and a contractor will do for the small one. It is closer to the opposite. On a large house there is slack — another room, another wall, somewhere to absorb a bad decision. On a two-bedroom cottage or a rear addition, there is no slack at all. Every choice shows, and you live with it at close range every day.

 

Four decades of these — additions, whole-house renovations, kitchens and new custom homes across Maryland, the District and Virginia. Most of them a normal size, on a normal street.
