Waterfront Estate  ·  Maryland

Main house · Guest cottage · Carriage barn · Port cochère · Pool · Interiors

Project Study

Five buildings. One conversation.

{{contact.first_name}}, the hardest thing about a property like this is not any one building. It is the space between them.

Anyone can draw a good house. Making a main house, a guest cottage and a carriage barn look like they grew up together on the same piece of land — and arranging them so that arriving feels like an event rather than a car park — is a different problem entirely. It is the same problem as a kitchen that never quite worked, just larger.

01  —  The main house
A shingled gambrel with a deep wraparound porch. The fanlight over the centre bay lines up with the drive, so the house announces itself from the moment you turn in.

02 — The port cochère

  

03 — The carriage barn
The port cochère does the quiet work — it turns a driveway into an arrival. The barn answers the main house in a different key: same gambrel, different roof, its own cupola.
04  —  The composition
This is the drawing that took the longest. Not either building — the relationship between them, and the shape of the gravel court that holds them apart.

05 — The guest cottage

  

06 — The pool terrace
The cottage gets its own pergola and its own patch of lawn, so guests are not living in the main house’s pocket.
07  —  Inside
Every coffer had to land somewhere. The ceiling grid sets out from the island and the door openings at once — get that wrong and you spend the rest of the job hiding it.

08 — The library

  

09 — The sun room
Two rooms, opposite intentions. One is built to keep the weather out and the light low. The other is a bay of glass on three sides. Both are the same house.

Why show you this

Not because your project is this. Because the method is the same.

Most of what we do is a good deal smaller than this — a rear addition, a kitchen that never worked, a house that needs one more bedroom. The estate got the same first meeting your project would: two questions about how you want to live, a survey of what is actually there, and drawings you can walk around in before anyone is holding a hammer.

There are four decades of these — most of them a normal size, on a normal street, for people who simply wanted their house to work.
